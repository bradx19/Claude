# Marketing Strategy Framework

The reasoning model behind a strategy proposal. Work the sections in order; each constrains the next. The goal is a chain of choices a reader can follow and challenge, not a menu of tactics.

## 1. Situation analysis

Answer honestly: where are we, and what's the real problem or opportunity?

- **Market:** size, growth, trends, timing. Is demand rising, flat, shifting?
- **Audience:** who buys/engages today, who we want, and the gap between them.
- **Competition:** who else competes for this attention, how they position, where they're strong and weak, what angles are unclaimed.
- **Internal:** current performance, assets, channels, capacity, and constraints.

Close with one sentence: the core challenge this strategy exists to solve. Everything after must serve it.

A lightweight SWOT or a "where we are / where we want to be / what's in the way" framing is enough. Don't pad; name the few things that actually matter.

## 2. Objectives

Translate the business goal into marketing outcomes that are specific, measurable, and time-bound.

- One **primary objective** (the thing success is judged on).
- 1 to 3 **supporting objectives** that enable it.
- Each with a target and a timeframe. "Increase awareness" is not an objective; "lift branded search volume 20% by end of Q3" is.

If the user can't give a number, propose a reasonable target with stated assumptions, and flag it for their confirmation.

## 3. Target audience

- Identify the priority segment(s); rank if more than one. Resist targeting everyone.
- For the top segment: their job/role or situation, the pain or desire that drives action, the trigger that makes them act now, and the objections that stop them.
- Capture their **language** (verbatim phrases from research, reviews, communities). This feeds positioning and all downstream copy.
- Note where they spend attention; this constrains the channel choice in section 5.

## 4. Positioning & core message

- **Core message:** the single idea you want to own in the audience's mind. One sentence.
- **Reason to believe:** the proof that makes it credible (results, mechanism, social proof, credentials).
- **Differentiation:** why this beats the alternative the audience would otherwise choose (including doing nothing).
- A simple positioning statement helps: *For [audience] who [need], [offer] is the [category] that [key benefit], because [reason to believe].*

Everything downstream ladders up to this. If a tactic doesn't reinforce the core message, question it.

## 5. Channel strategy

For each channel considered, decide its **role** and **justify it**:

- Role in the funnel: acquisition (reach new), nurture (build trust), conversion (drive action), retention (keep/expand).
- Why it fits *this* audience (from section 3) and *this* objective (section 2).
- Rough effort/return expectation.

Then make explicit choices:

- **Include** the channels that earn their place. Lead with the one or two that carry the objective.
- **Exclude** the rest, and say why (wrong audience, too slow, no capacity, poor fit). Exclusions show discipline.

Avoid the "be everywhere" trap. A focused two-to-three-channel plan executed well beats six done thinly.

## 6. Roadmap / phasing

Sequence the work. Two common shapes:

- **Phased** (for ongoing strategy): e.g. Phase 1 Foundation (fix tracking, core assets, positioning), Phase 2 Launch (primary channels live), Phase 3 Scale (double down on what works, expand). Give each phase a goal, a duration, and its key activities.
- **Calendar** (for a campaign): week-by-week activities with owners and dependencies, building to the key moment (launch, event, deadline).

Show dependencies (what must happen before what). A roadmap is about order and timing, not a flat task dump.

## 7. Budget & resourcing

- Split spend and effort across channels and phases.
- Tie allocation to expected contribution toward the objective (put money where the return is).
- Use ranges and state assumptions when exact figures aren't provided.
- Account for team capacity and skills, not just money; an under-resourced plan fails quietly.

## 8. Success metrics

- Choose KPIs that actually prove the objective, not vanity metrics.
- Include **leading indicators** (early signal: CTR, signups, pipeline) and **lagging indicators** (the outcome: revenue, retention, qualified leads).
- Set a target and a baseline for each, plus a **review cadence** (weekly/monthly) and what would trigger a change of course.
- Define "working" before launch so success isn't argued about after.

## 9. Risks & assumptions

- List the assumptions the plan rests on (demand level, channel performance, capacity, budget approval).
- Name the top risks and a brief mitigation or contingency for each.
- This makes the strategy honest and lets stakeholders pressure-test it before committing.

## The test of a good strategy

Can a reader trace every tactic back to an objective, and every objective back to the core challenge? Can they see what you chose *not* to do and why? If yes, it's a strategy. If it's a list of channels with budgets attached and no reasoning, it isn't.
